1

Test & calibrate the sensors

Before any wiring · ~½ day

Start here because the sensors are your long-lead item and your least-certain parts. Ohm each one across its two outer pins: a healthy A301 reads near-open at rest and drops sharply when you press the pad. Keep the four best. Then recalibrate every survivor — the old calibration factors are void now that the pads have been handled and remounted.

The one change that matters: last time you calibrated only up to about 15 N, then pitches loaded the pads far past that. This time load each sensor across the full range, up toward 80–110 N, so the fit covers where the real forces live.

  • Ohm all five sensors; keep the four best (rest = MΩ, pressed = drops hard).
  • Flash calibrate_sensors.ino, read on A2, log plateau vs. scale force.
  • Load to ~80–110 N, not 15 N. Levels like 10 / 30 / 50 / 80 / 110 N.
  • Fit with fit_calibration.py → record calFactor, R², RMSE per sensor.
2

Build the crimped harness

The reliability-critical step · ~1 day

This is the part that failed last time and the part to slow down on. In v1, bare wires soldered onto four adjacent pads slowly bridged together until three "independent" channels carried the same signal. The fix has three independent layers: non-adjacent pins, crimped contacts in plastic housings instead of bare solder blobs on the header, and conformal coating over everything.

Each channel is one voltage divider. The trick that keeps it clean: the sensor's signal wire and the resistor's top leg go into the same crimp contact, so the node is formed inside a plastic housing that plugs straight onto the analog pin — nothing floating in open space to migrate.

  • Practice crimp on scrap first — tug-test each; a bad crimp looks fine until it fails.
  • Sensor signal + resistor top leg into one female contact → houses onto the pin.
  • Solder the single GND bus joint (open space, low risk) or use a ferrule; heat-shrink it.
  • Wash every joint with 99% IPA — flux residue was a prime suspect in the v1 bridge.
  • Heat-shrink each crimp barrel; no bare metal left anywhere.
◈

GATE — isolation test (do not skip)

Flash isolation_test.ino. Press one pad at a time; every other channel must stay under 5% of the pressed one. A channel that follows = a bridge. Fix it now — this is the exact check that would have caught the v1 failure. Re-run it after coating, after potting, after stitching, and before every session.

3

Open the ball & carve the cavity

~2 hours

Unstitch the leather along one figure-eight seam and fold the cover back — this keeps the cover intact so you can re-lace it later (Yeh et al. cut theirs in half and sealed with polymer; unstitching is the more reversible route). Under the leather is tightly wound yarn around a cork-and-rubber pill. Dig a central pocket just big enough for the potted block, keeping the pill roughly centered so the ball still feels right. Save the yarn you remove — it goes back in for weight.

4

Place the sensors under the cover

~1 hour

Mount the four pads on the inside face of the leather at the exact finger contact points, following Yeh et al.'s method — the leather transmits finger force to the 0.2 mm pad while protecting it. Mark the spots from a real four-seam grip: index and middle on top across the seam, thumb underneath, ring on the side. Route each lead inward toward the central pocket, leaving a little slack so nothing pulls on a joint. Only the round sensing pad sits under the grip; bend the tail/pins inward, away from the film.

Watch the tails. The A301 tail is short but the pins are stiff — bend them inward once, gently, away from the sensing film, so nothing sits proud under the grip and no bend cracks the pad.
5

Coat, then pot the block at center

~½ day incl. cure

First, conformal-coat the whole harness — this seals every joint against the slow bridging that killed v1, and locks wires so epoxy pressure can't shift them. Let it cure, then run the isolation test again. Only then pot: nest the XIAO + LiPo + resistor hub into the central pocket and epoxy them as one block, with its center of mass at the ball's center. Pour thin layers, not one deep pour — a thick pour of epoxy gets genuinely hot as it cures and can damage the LiPo. Keep the sensing pads and the USB-C pigtail clear of epoxy.

◈

GATE — isolation test after coating, and again after potting

Coating and potting are exactly when a wire can shift or a pad crease. Press each pad, confirm the others stay under 5%. Catching a fault before the epoxy fully buries it is the difference between a 10-minute fix and a scrapped ball.

6

Ballast to weight & balance

~1 hour

Repack the saved yarn, then trim to the regulation 142–149 g with steel washers or BBs bedded in a little epoxy. The charge pigtail adds ~11 g on one side, so place the ballast diametrically opposite it to keep the center of mass centered. Verify with the roll test: roll the ball on a flat surface from random starts; a balanced ball settles randomly, an unbalanced one keeps putting the heavy spot down.

7

Re-lace, expose the pigtail, wall-test

~2 hours

Stitch the leather back with the figure-eight lacing (harvest lace from the spare ball if you run short). Bring the USB-C pigtail out through a small leather flap so you can recharge without opening the ball — recessed and taped flush, never proud, so it can't snag or get smashed. Before you unplug the charger for the last stitch, confirm the logger reads ≥ 4.15 V. Finally, borrow Yeh's durability check: throw it at a wall at game speed, then re-run the isolation test — if it still passes, the ball is field-ready.

The through-line: the isolation test appears in phases 2, 5, and 7, and before every data session. That single 60-second check is the habit that turns "hope it works" into "know it works." It is the one procedure the first ball never had.
